H1|1. Project Overview and Core Value
H2|1.1 Problem
P|In outpatient practice the physician spends a large part of the working day on the record rather than on the patient. Industry estimates commonly put manual documentation at 30-40% of working time; we treat that range as a working assumption to be confirmed by timed observation in a pilot, not as a measured fact. The consequences are visible in any busy clinic: notes written after hours from memory, structured fields left empty or copied from the previous visit, and a throughput ceiling set by paperwork rather than by clinical decision. The record is also where safety is lost: allergies documented in prose instead of structured fields cannot be checked downstream, so a prescription conflict surfaces only if a pharmacist happens to notice it.
H2|1.2 Solution
P|SmartMed is an ambient clinical documentation agent. It captures consultation audio inside the clinic's own network, separates speakers, converts the dialogue into a structured SOAP note coded against the national clinical ICD-10 revision, runs a deterministic safety check over the draft prescription, and hands the physician a pre-filled chart to approve. The system never signs a record; the physician does.
P|Scope of this submission: a research prototype for a student competition. Two students, no hospital data agreement, no dedicated GPU. Training uses public Chinese medical corpora and synthetic dialogues on Colab and an Apple Silicon laptop. Work that requires a clinical partner (real audio, ethics approval, classified-protection assessment) is on the development roadmap and is labelled as such throughout.
H2|1.3 Agent properties and track fit
P|The system behaves as an agent rather than a prompt wrapper: it perceives a continuous audio stream with voice activity detection, speaker roles and consultation phase tracking; it plans by calling discrete tools (ASR rescoring, retrieval over the drug and coding knowledge base, schema validator, rules engine) instead of leaving extraction to free-form generation; it keeps per-episode working memory plus a persistent patient summary read from the clinic's local store under explicit access control; and its only irreversible action is placing a draft in a review queue, since committing the record requires a physician's authenticated approval. That last point is what keeps clinical and legal responsibility with the clinician.
H2|1.4 Algorithmic contributions
B|Schema-constrained extraction. |Grammar-constrained decoding plus Pydantic validation produce a typed record that loads into a database without post-processing, and make silent format failure impossible.
B|Separation of probabilistic and deterministic stages. |The LLM drafts; a rules engine and a small clinical knowledge graph verify. A single model cannot be both generator and safety authority.
B|Hallucination suppression with measurable abstention. |Field-level provenance, retrieval grounding, and a confidence threshold that yields an empty field instead of an invention. The abstention rate is reported as a first-class metric.
B|Coding as constrained retrieval. |Codes are chosen only from retrieved candidates in the national clinical ICD-10 revision, with ICD-11 transition mapping, the most specific supported code preferred and symptom-level fallback.
H1|2. Algorithm Design and Model Selection
H2|2.1 Speech front end
P|Primary ASR is Paraformer (streaming and offline, FunASR); Whisper large-v3 is kept as a comparison candidate. The choice is decided by measured character error rate on consultation-like audio rather than by reputation, because outpatient rooms have HVAC noise, keyboard typing and overlapping speech from two or three people. Speaker diarization uses embeddings (CAM++) with online clustering, aligned to the transcript timeline so the extraction stage knows who said what. A hot-word list of drug names, departments and symptom collocations is applied through contextual biasing and n-best rescoring, since medical entities are exactly what generic ASR drops. Expected values, to be measured: CER ≤ 8%, diarization DER ≤ 12%, real-time factor ≤ 0.3 on one consumer GPU.
H2|2.2 Language model selection
P|Candidates were screened on Chinese medical competence, licence terms permitting private on-premise deployment, feasibility of 4-bit QLoRA within 16 GB VRAM, and reliability of structured output. This is a design decision; section 5.2 defines the ablation that tests it.

H2|2.3 Fine-tuning configuration
P|Training uses QLoRA with the base frozen in 4-bit NF4, double quantisation and paged optimiser state, LoRA adapters on the attention and feed-forward projections, bf16 compute, three epochs with early stopping on held-out field F1, and a 4096-token sequence budget. Full hyperparameters, the three-stage schedule (instruction SFT, rejection sampling on schema-valid outputs, then probability calibration) and the hardware actually available are listed in Appendix A. Calibration matters because the confidence thresholds in 2.5 and 2.6 are only meaningful on calibrated probabilities.
H2|2.4 Constrained decoding and validation
P|Top-level JSON is produced by grammar-constrained decoding, so output is always parseable; each section is validated by a Pydantic model with enums for units, frequency and severity, with one repair retry on failure. The failure policy matters more than the parser: a field the transcript does not support stays empty and is flagged for physician entry. The model is never asked to fill a gap plausibly.
H2|2.5 Diagnosis coding
P|The target is the ICD-10 national clinical revision 2.0 (国家临床版2.0) actually used for hospital coding in China, with a mapping layer to the ICD-11 transition version (过渡版) so the prototype survives the ongoing migration; targeting the international base edition alone would be wrong for this market. Coding is constrained retrieval plus classification: a bi-encoder indexes code titles, synonyms and inclusion terms, candidates are re-ranked, and the model chooses only among them, preferring the most specific code the transcript supports and falling back to a symptom code rather than presuming a disease. Below the coding threshold the field is left empty and the physician sees the top three candidates with their evidence spans.
H2|2.6 Hallucination suppression
P|We claim a low-hallucination pipeline, not a zero-hallucination one: no LLM system can guarantee the absence of invented content, and an absolute claim would not survive review. Each mechanism has a measurement in section 5.
B|Retrieval grounding. |Drug facts, contraindications and dose limits come from a curated local knowledge base built from published insert material and public guideline summaries, not from parametric memory.
B|Field-level provenance. |Every filled field stores the transcript span behind it; a field with no resolvable span is dropped before the review queue.
B|Confidence threshold with abstention. |Below threshold the agent outputs nothing for that field and marks it as requiring physician input.
B|Cross-field consistency and adversarial testing. |Allergy versus prescribed drug, age versus dose range, duplicate ingredients; plus a test set with deliberately missing information so the unsupported-field rate is measured rather than assumed.
H2|2.7 Deterministic safety engine
P|The safety layer is not an LLM. It is a rules engine over a small clinical knowledge graph covering allergy-drug contraindications, drug-drug interactions, dose ceilings by age and renal function, and pregnancy contraindications. Validation takes milliseconds and emits a full audit record with rule identifier and evidence. Rules are curated from published drug insert material, reviewed by the project's medical consultant, versioned in the repository and updated on a fixed cycle. The engine warns and blocks auto-commit; it never edits the physician's prescription.

Until such access exists, export is .docx and PDF plus a structured JSON payload.
H1|4. Data Strategy and Annotation
H2|4.1 Sources
P|Training data comes from three places. Public Chinese medical corpora (Huatuo-26M for coverage, DISC-MedLLM for instruction-formatted dialogue, MedDialog for conversational structure) supply language and clinical content but are not outpatient transcripts. A synthetic generator builds doctor-patient dialogues from SOAP templates with controlled variation in phrasing, incompleteness and interruption, and emits the structured record as ground truth, which is what makes field-level evaluation possible at zero data cost. Real audio is out of scope until an agreement with a clinic, ethics approval and patient informed consent exist, and is scheduled in the roadmap rather than in the current training set.
P|The limitation is stated plainly: public text and synthetic dialogues do not reproduce the acoustic and conversational properties of a real consultation room. The evaluation therefore reports domain-gap sensitivity explicitly, and the first post-competition milestone is a small recorded pilot under proper approvals.
H2|4.2 De-identification
P|Applied to every dataset we create or accept: direct identifiers removed, quasi-identifiers generalised (age bands, shifted dates, department instead of address), k-anonymity checked over three-field combinations, and a re-identification audit on a random sample by a second annotator. The procedure follows the Personal Information Protection Law and the health-data requirements of GB/T 35273, and a future clinical data agreement inherits these rules as contract terms.
H2|4.3 Annotation standard and quality control
P|A written specification defines each SOAP slot, the boundary between subjective and objective content, medication and allergy extraction, and code assignment including when no code may be assigned. Annotation runs in Label Studio with two independent annotators on a shared sample: a 50-item pilot round before any scaling, disagreements analysed and the specification revised, then a per-field-group Cohen's kappa ≥ 0.75 before the corpus is frozen. Clinical disagreements are settled by the project's medical consultant and the ruling is stored with the annotation record.
H2|4.4 Splits and leakage control
P|Group splits by dialogue and patient identifier keep near-duplicates out of different partitions. The held-out test set is excluded from synthesis prompts and few-shot examples, and every evaluation ships an n-gram overlap report between train and test, because a synthetic corpus can otherwise leak through template reuse.

H1|8. Regulatory and Compliance Path
P|Compliance is the main obstacle between a working prototype and a deployed product, so it belongs in the proposal rather than in a footnote.
B|Security and data. |A system holding patient records in a clinical setting falls under the classified protection regime, where level 3 (等级保护三级) is the practical expectation for hospital-connected systems, alongside the Cybersecurity, Data Security and Personal Information Protection laws. Assessment is external and paid, and we schedule it rather than assume it away.
B|Generative AI. |Public-facing generative services require algorithm filing under the applicable provisions. Our deployment model is a private internal tool for licensed clinicians, which narrows but does not eliminate the obligation; filing status is a roadmap task.
B|Device classification. |Software that only drafts documentation, with a licensed physician reviewing and signing every record, generally sits outside diagnostic decision-making device classification, whereas software recommending diagnosis or treatment is a medical device requiring NMPA registration. We deliberately keep the product on the documentation side of that line, and any move toward clinical recommendations triggers a formal classification request first.
P|This is a compliance plan written by students, not a legal opinion. The next concrete step is named: before any pilot on real data, obtain institutional review approval and a written classification assessment from the intended channel partner's regulatory team.

H1|11. Risk Assessment
P|Eight risks are registered with likelihood, impact and mitigation in Appendix F. Three shape our design decisions directly. Without real clinical audio the model learns synthetic speech patterns, which is why the speech front end is modular and the domain gap is reported rather than glossed over. Without an in-house clinician the annotation carries bias, which is why the specification is written, piloted, gated by kappa and adjudicated by the consultant. And with two students alongside coursework the only defence is scope: one pipeline, weekly checkpoints, and a supervisor review at every milestone.

H1|14. Business Model and Route to Market
P|The paying customer is an institution, not an individual physician, and the route is B2B2C through an existing health IT channel rather than direct hospital sales: a two-student team will not win a twelve-month hospital procurement cycle, but it can build a module that a HIS vendor or regional health platform adds to its installed base. Several domestic HIS vendors and internet health platforms fit this role; none is named as a committed partner. Revenue is a per-seat monthly subscription plus a one-time deployment and integration fee per institution, with volume pricing for platform partners. Entry is a free three-month pilot in one outpatient department, exchanged for timed measurements and annotation feedback, whose purpose is evidence rather than revenue. Open weights and a single local inference node keep the marginal cost per clinic low, which is what makes the subscription affordable for mid-sized private groups and community clinics that would never fund a hardware suite. No sales activity takes place during the competition phase, and every gate in section 8 precedes all of the above.
